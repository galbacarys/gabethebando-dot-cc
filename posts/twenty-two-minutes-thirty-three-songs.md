--- 
title: Twenty-Two Minutes, Thirty-three songs 
date: 07/25/2026
---

*Spoiler warning for Outer Wilds and Hadestown: The Musical. PLEASE go
watch/play both if you haven't.*


You wake up next to a campfire. You roast a marshmallow, then clamber your way
up through a clearing in a forest to an observatory adorably made of wood. You
get launch codes to a spaceship, dutifully waiting for you on top of the launch
platform next to where you awoke. And then, as you make your way to your ship,
a three-eyed statue stares directly at you and your memories replay in your
mind. Shrugging this weirdness off, you head to your spaceship.

Twenty-two minutes later, you hear ominous music echoing, filling the
periphery, almost narrowing your vision as it takes its hold on you. You don't
know it yet, but the haunting A-minor synthesizer bell chime is the death knell
of the star that has been slowly reddening overhead. You happen to look up at
just the exact moment when the angry glowing red orb collapses inward, becoming
a violent shimmering blue, before radiating outward. As it reaches you, the
scream of the stellar wind overwhelms everything and your vision goes white.

And then your memories replay in your mind.

They begin right at the moment of your demise, playing backwards and
accelerating until they get back to waking up next to that campfire.

And then you take a deep, gasping breath, and wake back up next to the
campfire. Everything is the same, except that you have just watched yourself
die. You, the player, are the main character now. The nameless creature in this
game, begins its loop anew, and as you begin exploring again, steering its
will-less body around like a puppet, you begin to learn what is happening
around you. You speak with other inhabitants of this tiny pocket solar system,
realizing with mounting horror that they _simply don't remember any of this
happening_. You're in a time loop, and the game doesn't seem to acknowledge it
at all. This is _Outer Wilds_.

This is fundamentally different than other video games, but it's not clear why
at first. Yes, of course, when you play a game and you die, you "respawn"
somewhere usually and keep on playing, but it's in the diegesis[^1] of the game
that you have "reset" back to that spot, continuing the gameplay as if you
hadn't died. Outer Wilds is different: the memory you, the person playing the
game, carry from iteration to iteration, _is_ the memory that carries forward.
Your character _remembers_ dying. They remember that they've done this again,
and again, and again. They've crashed into the sun, been blown apart by a
supernova, smashed into the side of a planet covered in sand, eaten by a giant
skeletal anglerfish.

The character's memory is _your_ memory. This is the thing I can't get out of
my head: the diegesis of the narrative sits outside it. You, the player, know
that time keeps resetting, and you remember the hours of problem solving that
come before the beautiful, violent, tragic end of this melancholy joy of a
game. But in the game...it's just 22 minutes. The fact that, if you know what
you're doing, you can bypass every puzzle and every moment of joyous discovery
in this game in just 22 minutes and shortcut straight to the end from the
moment you boot it up is so bizarre to me. It's so different than "cheating" in
a normal game where you might use a cheat code or a shortcut or a hack to gain
an advantage in the game. In Outer Wilds, the knowledge of what has happened
and what will happen are the only things that differentiate someone starting
the game from someone finishing the game. And all the while...

_another spoiler alert - seriously, PLEASE go play this game_

...there is nothing you can do to save everyone. The sun will explode, the
universe will go dark, and you will either choose to keep going, forcing
yourself to watch those final 22 minutes over and over again, not letting
yourself believe that this is really it, or finally, letting the game go dark
for the last time.


---

A man in a fabulous suit walks out on stage. He sings about an old story that
will be told again. This story is simple, it's about two pairs of lovers: a
poor musician and his muse, a heartless industrialist and his trophy. One is on
their way together, the other on their way apart. 

It's the story of Orpheus and Eurydice, the famous lovers of Greek myth:
Orpheus, son of a muse, courts Eurydice, a nymph of the forest. It's also the
story of Hades and Persephone, the king and queen of the underworld. In this
way, it constructs a beautiful and tragic parallel between the burgeoning love
of the former and the drifting apart of the latter. But something magical
happens over the course of _Hadestown_.

You see, to roughly quote the opening number again, _it's an old song_ that
will be sung again. And again. And again.

The audience participates in the diegesis of the cyclical nature of the story;
Broadway fans, notoriously obsessive over their favorite shows, will listen to
the ~~soundtrack~~cast recording[^2], all thirty-three songs, over and over and
over again. Each time, they will hear the story of Orpheus and Eurydice meeting
in the darkness of winter, the arrival of Persephone along with her summer
wine, the descent into a brutal winter, and the death of Orpheus's love. Hades
will sing to them of the world he has crafted for himself down below, full of
steel and fire, and of Orpheus's struggle to the Underworld. He will sing a
song so beautiful that it convinces Hades to give Eurydice a chance to leave,
even though she's sold her soul to him. And in the moment before they escape
the underworld, Orpheus's doubt will overcome him and he will break the promise
he made to Hades and to Eurydice, turning around to see her for one last moment
before she descends back into the Underworld forever.

I gasp every time I reach this point in the show, which I've now seen three
times. Of course I _know_ consciously that Orpheus will let his doubt overwhelm
him, and that the end of this beautiful experience is heartbreak, but I keep
watching because maybe, just maybe, _this_ time, somehow, Orpheus will take one
final step.

---

Outer Wilds is the only game that has ever made me cry. It's the only game that
has filled me with the existential void that comes with realizing that you are
a tiny speck -- _less than_ a tiny speck -- in this vast, beautiful, weird,
unfathomable universe. And it is a game about accepting the void, that as a
tiny speck in this vast, beautiful, weird, unfathomable universe, you will die.
The whole world you know around you, eventually, will die. And yet there is so
much joy and discovery and triumph to be had, even as we watch the minutes
dwindle away and the end of our cycle comes for us.

And Hadestown does the same thing: the springtime of finding love, the summer
of revelry, the autumn of the approaching tragedy of death, and the winter of
death itself. There is a deep question in there, of _why_ we subject ourselves
to the tragedy we know will someday come just to feel alive for a fleeting
moment.

And while I think there's a clearly pessimistic reading of Outer Wilds, namely
that your continued repetition of the experience is just forcing the entirety
of your world to experience its death over and over again, in the vain hope
that you will prevent it somehow or at least stop it from coming for a little
longer, I think taken in concert with the beauty of the twin loves of Orpheus
and Eurydice and of Hades and Persephone the meaning becomes clear. Love is
beautiful, life is beautiful, and discovering just how much one can love and
live is reason enough. There is a deep love that permeates Outer Wilds; a love
for play, in the way the little spaceship is goofy and hard to control and the
silly ways that the other Hearthians (the alien race you are a member of) talk
about and respond to what's happening around them. And there is also a love and
reverence for the act of exploration itself, the act of taking risks and ending
up somewhere you did not expect in the slightest. In this way, I think
Hadestown and Outer Wilds are saying the same thing: the great joy of life is
to explore, and even given the knowledge that someday your winding path will
lead to an end you cannot avoid, the journey is not only worth it, but
essential to being human.


[^1]: Diegesis is this sort of fuzzy concept that roughly means "in the logic
    of the story". A really famous example is the diegetic (adjective form of
    the word) menuing you do on the Pip-Boy in the Fallout games.

[^2]: An old friend of mine will rip me a new one if she ever discovers I
    called the recording of a Broadway show a "soundtrack"
